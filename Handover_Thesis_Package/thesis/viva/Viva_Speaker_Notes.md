# Viva Speaker Notes: What to Say on Each Slide

**Target length:** 18–20 minutes for slides 1–21. Backup slides are only for questions.
**How to use:** these notes are also in the PPT's speaker-notes pane (Presenter View). Don't read them word for word; learn the first and last sentence of each slide and speak the middle in your own words.
**Pace:** about 130 words a minute. The red element on each slide is the one thing to point at.

## Slide 1 – Title  *(0:30)*

Good morning. My project is titled "AI-Driven Transformation in Banking GRC: Implications for Workforce Management in UK and EU Banks", carried out under the guidance of Dr. Ramya J. In one line: I studied what happens to the people in banks' governance, risk and compliance functions when AI comes in, and what management should do about it.

## Slide 2 – AI is entering bank risk and compliance faster than people can govern it  *(1:00)*

Let me start with why this matters. The Bank of England and FCA survey of 2024 found that 75% of UK financial firms already use AI. But look at the number in red: 46% of firms say they only partly understand the AI they use, and 57% say talent and skills are a constraint. So adoption is not the problem. The problem is that the technology is moving faster than the people who have to govern it. In risk and compliance that gap is dangerous, because these are the people who answer to regulators.

## Slide 3 – This is a management problem, and regulation makes it urgent  *(1:00)*

When AI takes over routine monitoring and reporting, managers face four decisions: which roles to redesign, reduce or create; whether to build, buy or borrow skills; how to shape the team; and how to get staff to trust AI appropriately, neither blindly nor not at all. On the right are four regulations. DORA requires board and staff ICT competence. The EU AI Act requires AI literacy and human oversight. The PRA's SS1/23 requires skilled model validators, and SM&CR makes named senior managers personally accountable. Together they make competence and human oversight a legal obligation, not just an HR preference. A bank that automates a control without people who can challenge it hasn't removed the risk; it has moved it.

## Slide 4 – Objectives  *(0:45)*

My primary objective was to examine how AI is reshaping workforce decisions in GRC (roles, skills, staffing and employee adaptation) and to build a practical framework for management. The five secondary objectives follow from it: which activities are automated or augmented, how regulation shapes oversight and competence, where the skill gaps are, how employees respond, and finally, in red, the framework. The first four build the evidence; the fifth turns it into something a bank can use.

## Slide 5 – Three bodies of research, and none of them looks at the GRC workforce  *(1:15)*

I reviewed eight core sources in three streams. First, technology acceptance and trust: Davis's Technology Acceptance Model says people use technology they find useful and easy to use, and Glikson and Woolley show that trust in AI depends on transparency and reliability. Second, automation and work: Autor argues that automation replaces routine tasks but complements judgement, so jobs are recomposed rather than eliminated; Frey and Osborne show routine work is most exposed; Cascio and Montealegre frame technology change as an HR and organisation-design problem; and Brougham and Haar show that employees who feel replaceable become less committed and more likely to leave. Third, RegTech: Arner and colleagues, and Buckley and colleagues, show that technology and regulation are reshaping compliance work itself. The gap, in red, is the overlap: none of them studies workforce management inside regulated banking GRC.

## Slide 6 – The literature gave four propositions to test  *(0:45)*

From that review I set four propositions. P1, from Autor and Frey and Osborne: routine GRC tasks are automated first and judgement work is augmented. P2, from Buckley and colleagues and the regulations: regulation turns competence and oversight into obligations. P3, from Cascio and Montealegre: AI needs a hybrid profile of technical and regulatory skills. P4 is marked "emergent" because it was not in my original plan. It came out of the interviews: losing experienced people's knowledge is a hidden risk of restructuring.

## Slide 7 – A sequential exploratory mixed-methods design  *(1:00)*

My design has three steps. Step one, listen: written interviews with GRC practitioners and senior professionals gave ten themes, and a questionnaire measured how strongly practitioners agree with them. Step two, test: I turned each testable theme into a hypothesis and tested it on public industry data. Step three, compare: I triangulated everything against the four propositions and built the framework. The key point is this. The interviews decided *what* to test; the public data showed *whether it holds* across the industry. The public datasets are independent sources and were not produced from my interviews. This is a recognised design called sequential exploratory mixed methods: qualitative first, quantitative second.

## Slide 8 – Two kinds of evidence  *(1:00)*

On the primary side, the interview form had eleven open questions in five sections, done in writing because the respondents were senior and busy. The questionnaire had 25 Likert items in seven constructs, adapted from Davis, Glikson and Woolley, and Brougham and Haar. Sampling was purposive with snowball referral, because I needed people with real exposure to AI in GRC. On the secondary side, four public datasets each answer a different question. D1, Eurostat, covers the economy: AI adoption in 27 EU countries. D2 covers the banks: annual reports of large UK and EU banks for FY2022 and FY2025, with 19 banks having both years. D3 covers the job market: 163 UK GRC job adverts. D4 covers the profession: published surveys from the Bank of England and FCA, the IIA and Thomson Reuters.

## Slide 9 – Every hypothesis traces back to something practitioners said  *(1:15)*

This slide is the bridge in my design. Take the first row. Practitioners said AI is spreading but still pilot-heavy, and the questionnaire showed strong agreement that roles will change, at 4.33. That became H5a and H8: is AI adoption actually rising? Row two: routine work is being absorbed, which became H10a on AI mentions in bank reports and H6 on the skill mix in job adverts. Practitioners said humans must be able to challenge AI, which became H10b: are banks setting up AI governance bodies? Regulation is felt unevenly, which became H10d: do UK and EU banks differ? Hybrid skills became H5b, H6 and H7. And the row in red: practitioners rated training adequacy lowest, at 3.33, which became H9 and H10c: is reskilling keeping up with AI? Every test exists because a theme pointed to it.

## Slide 10 – Each test was chosen by the shape of the data  *(1:15)*

I chose each test by asking what the data looks like. Where the same countries or banks are measured twice, I used a paired t-test, with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a check that doesn't assume normality, because bank data is small and skewed. Friedman covers all four Eurostat years. Where the outcome is yes or no for the same banks in two years, does the bank name an AI governance body, I used the exact McNemar test. For two independent, skewed groups, UK versus EU banks or salaries, I used Mann–Whitney U. For yes/no skill flags across two groups of adverts, I used chi-square, or Fisher where counts are small, with a Holm correction because I tested six skills at once. Where I only have published percentages, I used z-tests. For the strength of a link, I used Pearson, Spearman and a simple regression. Finally, in red: the questionnaire is descriptive only. On a small purposive sample, significance tests are not meaningful, so I compare means with the neutral point of 3 and let the public data carry the statistics.

## Slide 11 – Practitioners: routine work goes, judgement and knowledge matter more  *(1:00)*

Now the findings, starting with what practitioners told me. Three quotes capture it. "GRC testing roles have disappeared": routine execution work goes first. "C-level execs really do not understand what the AI is, and thus are likely to simply trust it": the people formally accountable may understand AI least, which is a real risk under SM&CR. And "too much business knowledge exists in people's heads… prioritise knowledge capture before any significant workforce reductions". That last quote became proposition P4. Underneath are the ten themes grouped by objective.

## Slide 12 – Questionnaire: tools are easy to use but hard to see into, and training lags  *(1:00)*

All seven constructs sit above the neutral point of 3. Ease of use is highest, at 4.00. But the gaps tell the real story. On the right: the tools are easy to use, 4.33, but understanding what the tool is doing is only 3.33. That is a usability–transparency gap, exactly the transparency dimension of trust in Glikson and Woolley. And while practitioners say data skills are more important, at 4.00, training adequacy is the lowest skills item, at 3.33. I report these as means and standard deviations only, descriptive and not generalised.

## Slide 13 – AI adoption is surging everywhere (H5a, H8, H10a supported)  *(1:00)*

The public data confirm the first theme from three independent directions. On the left, Eurostat: AI adoption among large EU firms nearly doubled, from 27.5% in 2021 to 53.6% in 2025, and it rose in all 27 countries, p below 0.0001. UK financial firms using AI rose from 58% to 75%, p = 0.008. On the right, each grey line is one bank. AI mentions per 10,000 words rose about tenfold between FY2022 and FY2025, and in every one of the 19 paired banks. That is why the Wilcoxon statistic is at its maximum possible value of 190. Three sources, one direction.

## Slide 14 – Governance structures are growing faster than governance practice  *(1:00)*

On regulation and governance, banks are clearly building structures. The share of paired banks naming a dedicated AI governance body (an AI committee, a chief data or AI officer, a responsible-AI office) went from 5% to 42%. Seven banks added one and none dropped one; the exact McNemar test gives p = 0.016. Mentions of the AI Act, DORA and SS1/23 went from almost zero to routine. But in red: broader AI-governance wording, such as human oversight, explainability and model risk, did not rise significantly, p about 0.07. And UK banks talk about AI and AI governance more than EU banks, consistent with the UK's accountability regime. So structures are ahead of practice, which matches the practitioner view that oversight exists on paper but is not yet embedded.

## Slide 15 – Skills are shifting, but reskilling is not keeping up  *(1:00)*

In the job adverts, AI-exposed roles ask for data skills about twice as often, 51% versus 25%; automation nearly four times as often; and cyber skills more often. But they ask for regulatory skills far less often, 40% versus 78%. All of these are significant after Holm correction. That is the hybrid gap: employers hire technical profiles for AI work, while practitioners and regulators say regulatory judgement still matters. Roles with AI in the title pay a median of £90,000 against £60,000, but that group is small, so I treat it as indicative. And in red: bank reports' reskilling mentions are not linked to AI intensity, with a Spearman rho of minus 0.16, not significant. AI talk has grown far faster than reskilling talk.

## Slide 16 – Hypothesis scorecard  *(0:45)*

Here is the full scorecard. Most hypotheses are supported: adoption, the ICT-skills link, the skill mix, the governance bodies and the rise in AI, reskilling and regulation disclosure. Four are partly supported: conditional trust, the pay premium, the skills constraint and the UK–EU difference. Two are not supported, in red: AI-governance wording did not rise, and reskilling is not linked to AI intensity. I want to stress that the unsupported results are findings too. They show exactly where banks are lagging.

## Slide 17 – Triangulation: all four propositions hold  *(0:45)*

Bringing the three streams together: P1, routine work automated first, is supported by the interviews, the job descriptions and the adoption tests. P2, regulation creating obligations, is supported in regulatory texts and governance structures, but only partly in day-to-day practice. P3, the hybrid skill profile, is supported by the interviews, the adverts and the surveys. P4, knowledge loss, is supported qualitatively, because public data cannot measure tacit knowledge directly, so I present it as an emergent contribution. Where practitioners and public data agree, I'm confident; where only practitioners speak, I say so.

## Slide 18 – AI changes the composition of GRC work, not just its volume  *(0:45)*

This is the first output: the GRC role-impact map. Automated: data consolidation, reconciliation and first-level alert triage. Augmented: investigation, KYC judgement and model validation. Newly created: AI governance leads, AI model-risk validators and supervisors of AI agents. And in red, human-retained: interpreting regulatory intent, challenging decisions and final accountability. Jobs are recomposed, not simply removed, which is Autor's argument, now observed inside a regulated bank.

## Slide 19 – A seven-stage framework, in which knowledge protection comes before restructuring  *(1:00)*

The second output, and the answer to objective five, is a seven-stage framework: diagnose which activities are exposed; protect knowledge; redesign roles; build oversight competence; reskill and source through build, buy or borrow; secure trust and adoption; and lead change and retain talent. The most important design choice is the order, shown in red. Knowledge protection comes before any restructuring, because the experts whose routine work is automated are the same people needed to validate the AI. Three enablers run across all stages: data quality, a clear accountability map, and tracking workforce indicators alongside AI indicators.

## Slide 20 – Recommendations, conclusion and limits  *(1:00)*

My recommendations go to three audiences. GRC leaders should map exposure at the level of activities, not job titles, and capture knowledge before cutting roles. HR should close the training gap with role-tiered AI literacy and create visible career paths for junior staff. Boards and senior managers should close the AI-understanding gap at the top and pair every AI plan with a people plan. On limits: the primary sample is small and purposive, so it supports analytical rather than statistical generalisation; disclosure counts measure words, not actions; I have 19 paired banks and one UK job board. Future work should use larger surveys, track banks over time and add EU job data. My conclusion: AI in GRC succeeds only if the people who answer for it can understand and challenge it.
