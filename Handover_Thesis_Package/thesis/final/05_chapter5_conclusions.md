# CHAPTER 5 - CONCLUSIONS

## 5.1 SUMMARY OF FINDINGS

The findings are summarised below against each secondary objective.

**Objective (i): activities automated or augmented, and role demand.**

- Rules-based automation of GRC work is established: data collection and consolidation, reconciliation, management information, rules-based control monitoring and first-level alert triage. AI is spreading quickly but is often still at pilot stage in practitioners' own areas (T1–T3).
- Public data confirm the spread. AI adoption among large EU enterprises roughly doubled from 27.5% in 2021 to 53.6% in 2025, and rose in every country (H5a). The share of UK financial firms using AI rose from 58% to 75% (H8). AI references in bank annual reports rose about tenfold between FY2022 and FY2025, increasing in all 19 paired banks (H10a).
- Execution and testing roles contract first, while review, investigation and AI governance roles grow. P1 is supported.

**Objective (ii): regulation, oversight and competence.**

- DORA, the EU AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR convert competence and human oversight into obligations that must be evidenced.
- References to these instruments in bank reports went from near zero to routine (H10a). The share of banks naming a dedicated AI governance body rose from 5% to 42% (H10b). UK banks discuss AI and AI governance more than EU banks (H10d, partly supported).
- Broader AI-governance wording did not rise significantly, and practitioners rated the clarity of oversight requirements in practice (3.67) below the knowledge of accountable people (4.00). The understanding of AI among accountable senior managers is the critical weak point (T5).
- P2 is supported in regulatory texts and governance structures, and partly in day-to-day practice.

**Objective (iii): skill gaps and reskilling.**

- A hybrid profile combining data, AI, cyber and automation skills with regulatory judgement is required (T7). AI-exposed adverts ask for data skills about twice as often (51% vs 25%), automation skills nearly four times as often (49% vs 13%) and cyber skills more often (19% vs 6%), but regulatory skills less often (40% vs 78%) (H6). Countries with more ICT specialists adopt more AI (H5b).
- Practitioners rate data and judgement skills as more important (4.00), but training adequacy lowest among skills items (3.33). Reskilling disclosure in bank reports rose only modestly and was not linked to AI intensity (H10c). Banks' workforce response lags their AI ambition.
- A pay premium is visible for AI-titled roles (median £90,000 vs £60,000) but not for broadly AI-exposed roles (H7, partly supported).
- The loss of institutional knowledge held by long-serving experts is the major hidden risk of restructuring (T8). P3 is supported; P4 is supported by qualitative evidence as an emergent contribution.

**Objective (iv): employee behavioural responses.**

- AI tools are perceived as easy to use (4.00) and useful (3.58), but easier to operate than to understand (item 11: 4.33 vs item 12: 3.33).
- Trust is conditional. Practitioners keep a human check in place even when they rate outputs as reliable, and comfort in relying on AI output without re-performing the work is the more dispersed rating.
- Change is accepted as inevitable (item 20: 4.33), while personal concern about replacement varies widely. Anxiety is concentrated among junior staff, and business change management is described as weak (T10).

**Objective (v): framework.**

- A seven-stage, sequenced workforce transformation framework was developed (Section 4.9): diagnose, protect knowledge, redesign roles, build oversight competence, reskill and source, secure trust and adoption, and lead change and retain talent. Knowledge protection comes before any restructuring.

## 5.2 SUGGESTIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

### 5.2.1 For GRC leadership

1. **Map AI exposure at the level of activities, not job titles.** Use the role-impact map (Table 4.25) to classify each team's activities as automated, augmented, newly created or human-retained before changing headcount.
2. **Capture knowledge before reducing headcount.** Identify knowledge-critical roles, then codify procedures, record decision rationales and set up mentoring. Do not make material reductions until this is complete.
3. **Rewrite job descriptions towards review, challenge and system ownership.** Create AI governance, model validation and AI assurance roles. Keep developmental entry-level roles so that future experts can still be trained.
4. **Make human oversight operational.** Define who reviews which AI output, how disagreements are recorded and how outputs can be overridden. Provide explainability at the point of use.

### 5.2.2 For HR leadership

1. **Close the training gap.** Training adequacy was the lowest-rated skills item, and reskilling disclosure has not kept pace with AI. Build tiered AI-literacy programmes for staff, overseers, model owners and the board, and keep records as compliance evidence.
2. **Develop hybrid skills through domain-specific learning.** Practitioners asked for dedicated learning modules for each regulation within each GRC domain, and for prompts embedded in AI tools.
3. **Use a build, buy and borrow strategy deliberately.** Build hybrid skills internally, buy scarce AI-risk and model-risk specialists, and borrow vendor capacity under DORA third-party controls.
4. **Create visible career pathways.** Show junior staff how processing roles lead to investigation, quality assurance, data and AI governance roles. Communicate role impact early and openly.

### 5.2.3 For boards and accountable senior managers

1. **Close the understanding gap at the top.** Senior managers accountable under SM&CR and DORA should receive targeted training on AI capabilities, limits and failure modes, so that they can challenge AI-driven outputs rather than simply trust them.
2. **Link AI strategy to workforce strategy.** Every AI deployment plan in GRC should include a matching people plan covering skills, oversight roles, knowledge capture and communication.
3. **Track workforce indicators alongside AI indicators.** Monitor training completion, override rates, staff turnover in affected teams and engagement among junior staff, not only automation savings.

## 5.3 CONCLUSIONS

This study set out to examine how AI-driven transformation in UK and EU banking GRC is reshaping workforce management decisions, and to develop a practical framework for managing that change. Combining practitioner interviews, a questionnaire, regulatory analysis and statistical tests on four public datasets, it reaches four conclusions.

First, AI in GRC recomposes roles rather than removing them. Routine, rules-based work is absorbed first, while interpretation, challenge and accountability stay with people. This is consistent with Autor's (2015) distinction between substitution and complementarity, now observed in a regulated banking function.

Second, regulation makes workforce capability auditable. DORA, the AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR do not prescribe headcount, but they do prescribe who must understand what and who is answerable. Banks are responding with governance structures and regulatory language faster than with day-to-day oversight practice.

Third, the workforce response lags the technology. AI adoption and AI disclosure have risen sharply and consistently, while reskilling has grown far more slowly and is not matched to AI intensity. Practitioners see this gap directly in the adequacy of training.

Fourth, the most serious risk is hidden. When experienced staff leave before their knowledge is captured, the bank loses the very capability it needs to validate and govern AI. This emergent finding extends the automation literature and places knowledge protection at the start of the proposed framework.

The seven-stage framework gives GRC and HR leaders a sequenced, regulation-aware plan. If banks follow it, AI can improve compliance effectiveness without hollowing out the expertise on which compliance depends.

## 5.4 DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

1. **Larger practitioner samples.** A larger, stratified questionnaire across the three lines of defence and both jurisdictions would allow formal tests of H1–H4, reliability analysis and comparisons between practitioner and managerial respondents.
2. **Longitudinal tracking.** Repeating the bank-report and job-advert analysis annually would show whether reskilling disclosure catches up with AI disclosure, and how roles evolve as the AI Act's high-risk obligations take effect.
3. **EU labour-market data.** Extending the job-advert analysis to EU job boards would allow direct UK–EU comparison of skill demand and pay.
4. **Case studies inside banks.** With institutional access, case studies could link AI deployments to actual headcount, training spend, override rates and staff turnover.
5. **Knowledge loss.** The knowledge-loss risk identified in this study merits dedicated research on how GRC expertise can be captured and transferred during automation.
6. **Agentic AI.** As agentic AI begins to handle multi-step KYC and AML work, research is needed on the design of human-in-the-loop supervisory roles and the skills they require.
