# CHAPTER 1 - INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 RESEARCH BACKGROUND

Banks in the United Kingdom (UK) and the European Union (EU) are investing substantially in Artificial Intelligence (AI) across their Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC) functions. GRC covers the activities through which a bank governs itself, identifies and controls risk, and demonstrates compliance with law and regulation. It includes compliance monitoring and advisory, financial crime and anti-money laundering (AML) operations, know-your-customer (KYC) and customer due diligence (CDD), operational and ICT risk, model risk and validation, regulatory reporting and internal audit. These functions are labour-intensive, data-heavy and governed by detailed rules, which makes them natural candidates for automation and AI.

AI is now applied to regulatory monitoring, customer and transaction screening, risk scoring, control testing, document analysis and compliance reporting. The joint Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) survey of AI in UK financial services found that 75% of firms already use AI, with a further 10% planning to do so within three years (Bank of England & FCA, 2024). One third of AI use cases are third-party implementations, and risk and compliance is among the business areas with the highest share of such implementations, at 64%. The same survey found that 46% of firms have only a partial understanding of the AI technologies they use, and that insufficient talent and access to skills is a large or medium constraint for more than half of firms. Adoption is therefore moving faster than the capability needed to govern it.

Generative AI and large language models have widened the range of GRC tasks that can be automated or assisted. Earlier rules-based automation handled structured, repeatable work such as data aggregation and reconciliation. Newer tools can draft case summaries, map regulatory obligations and review documents. This brings AI closer to work that was previously considered judgement-based and raises new questions about who checks AI output and who is accountable for it.

The regulatory environment makes these questions urgent. The EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) requires the management body to maintain sufficient knowledge of ICT risk and requires ICT security and resilience training for all staff. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act introduces an AI-literacy duty and requires effective human oversight of high-risk AI systems. In the UK, PRA Supervisory Statement SS1/23 sets expectations for model risk management, including the skills of those who develop and validate models, and the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) holds named individuals personally accountable for decisions that are increasingly AI-assisted. Staff competence and effective human oversight have therefore become regulatory conditions, not merely HR objectives.

## 1.2 IDENTIFIED PROBLEM

The business case for AI in GRC is usually framed around cost efficiency and faster regulatory response. In practice, the return depends less on the capability of the technology than on the readiness of the workforce that must operate alongside it. When AI absorbs routine monitoring and reporting work, management must decide:

- which GRC roles are redesigned, reduced or newly created;
- which skills must be built internally, bought in the market or borrowed from vendors;
- how teams should be structured and staffed; and
- how employees can be brought to trust and use AI-enabled controls appropriately rather than over-relying on them or working around them.

These are workforce management decisions, and at present they are taken with little empirical guidance specific to regulated banking. A bank that automates a compliance process without ensuring that its people can understand and challenge the output has not reduced its risk; it has relocated it. The problem addressed by this study is therefore a management problem rather than a technical one: how GRC and HR leaders in UK and EU banks should plan role design, reskilling and employee adaptation as AI is introduced, while keeping compliance effective and employees engaged.

## 1.3 NEED FOR THE STUDY

The study is needed for four reasons.

First, the scale of change is significant and immediate. AI adoption in UK financial services is already widespread, and publicly advertised AI risk and compliance roles grew by 187% year-on-year while general technology hiring contracted, with financial services and banking among the leading hiring sectors (Global Skill Development Council, 2026).

Second, regulation now requires banks to demonstrate that their people are competent to supervise AI. Under DORA, the AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR, failures of competence or oversight become regulatory findings and, in the UK, personal exposures for senior managers.

Third, existing research does not address this intersection. The RegTech literature explains how technology is reshaping compliance but treats the workforce only implicitly. The work and employment literature explains how automation recomposes jobs, but across industries rather than within regulated banking functions. The technology acceptance and trust literature explains individual adoption largely through experimental rather than high-stakes settings.

Fourth, GRC and HR leaders need practical guidance. A sequenced, evidence-based framework linking AI adoption to role redesign, reskilling and employee adaptation would help banks avoid two common failures: cutting experienced staff before their knowledge is captured, and deploying AI faster than the organisation can oversee it.

## 1.4 OBJECTIVES OF THE STUDY

### 1.4.1 Primary objective

To examine how AI-driven transformation in the Governance, Risk and Compliance functions of UK and EU banks is reshaping workforce management decisions on role structure, skill requirements, staffing and employee adaptation, and to develop a practical framework enabling banking management to plan GRC workforce transformation while sustaining compliance effectiveness and employee engagement.

### 1.4.2 Secondary objectives

i. To map which GRC activities are being automated or augmented by AI in UK and EU banks, and how this is changing the demand for compliance and risk roles.

ii. To analyse how UK and EU regulatory requirements (DORA, the EU AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR) shape role design, human oversight and competence obligations within GRC functions.

iii. To identify emerging skill gaps and reskilling strategies in GRC functions, and to assess how they are being addressed by management.

iv. To examine employee behavioural responses to AI adoption in GRC (perceived usefulness, trust, perceived replaceability and readiness to adapt) and their implications for adoption and retention.

v. To develop an integrated workforce transformation framework and recommendations for GRC and HR leadership in UK and EU banks.

## 1.5 SCOPE OF THE STUDY

The study covers GRC functions in banks and banking service providers, including global capability centres, operating in the UK and the EU. Within GRC it covers compliance, risk, financial crime, model risk, operational and ICT risk, regulatory reporting and internal audit, together with the GRC technology and HR roles that support them.

The regulatory scope covers DORA, the EU AI Act as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI (2026), PRA SS1/23 and SM&CR. Related instruments that bear directly on GRC competence, such as the FCA Consumer Duty, the EU Anti-Money Laundering Regulation and the ECB supervisory priorities for 2026–28, are considered where relevant.

The time frame for secondary data is 2021 to 2025, covering the period in which generative AI reached the market and DORA and the AI Act were adopted. Bank disclosures compare financial year 2022 with financial year 2025.

The study does not assess the technical performance of AI models, does not use confidential bank data, and does not evaluate any individual bank. It is conducted independently and not as a company project.

## 1.6 DELIVERABLES

The study delivers:

1. An analysis of how AI adoption is changing GRC activities, role structures and skill demand in UK and EU banks.
2. A mapping of UK and EU regulatory requirements (DORA, the EU AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR) to workforce competence and human oversight obligations.
3. A statistical assessment, on public industry data, of the hypotheses arising from the practitioner evidence.
4. A GRC role-impact map classifying activities and roles as automated, augmented, newly created or human-retained.
5. An assessment of skill gaps and reskilling strategies, including build, buy and borrow options for GRC talent.
6. An analysis of employee behavioural responses (perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust and perceived replaceability) and their implications for adoption.
7. An integrated seven-stage workforce transformation framework.
8. Evidence-based recommendations for GRC and HR leadership in UK and EU banks.

## 1.7 ORGANISATION OF THE REPORT

Chapter 1 introduces the background, problem, need, objectives, scope and deliverables of the study. Chapter 2 reviews the literature on technology acceptance, trust, workplace automation, RegTech and employee perceptions of AI, sets out the regulatory context and identifies the research gap. Chapter 3 describes the research methodology, including the sequential exploratory design, data sources, instruments, sampling, propositions, hypotheses and statistical tools. Chapter 4 presents the analysis and interpretation of the interview, questionnaire and secondary data, the statistical hypothesis tests, the triangulation of evidence, the GRC role-impact map and the workforce transformation framework. Chapter 5 summarises the findings, presents recommendations and conclusions, and suggests directions for future research.
