# CHAPTER 2 - LITERATURE SURVEY

## 2.1 INTRODUCTION

AI in Governance, Risk and Compliance has been studied mainly as a technological and regulatory development. The workforce consequences of that development have been examined mostly in the general management literature rather than in a banking compliance setting. This chapter draws the two streams together. It reviews eight sources from management, organisational behaviour and financial regulation, grouped into four themes: technology acceptance and trust (Section 2.2), automation and the recomposition of work (Section 2.3), RegTech and the transformation of compliance (Section 2.4), and employee perceptions of AI (Section 2.5). Section 2.6 sets out the regulatory context that makes workforce capability an obligation in UK and EU banks. Section 2.7 summarises the review, Section 2.8 identifies the research gap, and Section 2.9 links the literature to the objectives and propositions of the study.

## 2.2 TECHNOLOGY ACCEPTANCE AND TRUST IN AI

### 2.2.1 Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989)

Davis (1989) established the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). Through validated measurement scales, he showed that an individual's use of a technology is determined by two beliefs: perceived usefulness, the degree to which a person believes the system will improve job performance, and perceived ease of use, the degree to which using it will be free of effort. Perceived usefulness was the stronger determinant of use, and ease of use acted largely through it: a system that is easy to use but does not help the user do the job is unlikely to be adopted.

TAM is relevant to GRC because AI tools are only valuable if compliance and risk professionals actually rely on them. A tool that is technically capable but perceived as unhelpful will be worked around, leaving the bank paying for both the technology and the manual process it was meant to replace. The present study adapts Davis's usefulness and ease-of-use constructs for the questionnaire (Constructs A and B).

### 2.2.2 Human trust in AI (Glikson and Woolley, 2020)

Glikson and Woolley (2020) reviewed two decades of empirical research on human trust in AI. They concluded that the successful integration of AI into organisations depends critically on workers' trust. They distinguished cognitive trust, which is shaped by the tangibility, transparency, reliability and immediacy behaviours of the technology, from emotional trust, which is associated with anthropomorphism. Their review also showed that the way AI is represented (as a robot, a virtual agent or embedded in software) affects how trust develops.

Two points from this review are central to the present study. First, transparency and reliability are separate drivers: a user may find outputs reliable but still not understand how they were produced. In GRC, where staff must be able to explain and defend a decision to a regulator or auditor, this distinction matters. Second, Glikson and Woolley observed that the evidence base relies heavily on short-term, small-sample experimental studies, and that trust is likely to develop differently in longer-term, high-stakes field settings. Regulated banking GRC is such a setting. The questionnaire's trust construct (Construct C) is adapted from this review.

## 2.3 AUTOMATION AND THE RECOMPOSITION OF WORK

### 2.3.1 Substitution and complementarity (Autor, 2015)

Autor (2015) examined the history and future of workplace automation and asked why, despite centuries of labour-saving technology, there are still so many jobs. He argued that automation substitutes for labour in some tasks while complementing it in others. Machines take over routine, codifiable tasks, but this raises the value of the tasks that remain, particularly those requiring judgement, problem solving, adaptability and interpersonal skill. Occupations are therefore recomposed rather than simply eliminated, and the gains from automation depend on how well human and machine tasks are combined.

This distinction underpins the view that AI in GRC leads mainly to role redesign rather than to wholesale job loss. A transaction-monitoring analyst whose first-level alert triage is automated does not disappear; the role shifts towards investigating the alerts that remain and documenting decisions. Autor's framework is the basis for the first research proposition (P1): routine GRC tasks are automated first, while judgement-intensive tasks are augmented.

### 2.3.2 Susceptibility to computerisation (Frey and Osborne, 2017)

Frey and Osborne (2017) estimated the probability of computerisation for 702 occupations, using expert task assessments and a classification model applied to occupational task data. They concluded that about 47% of total United States employment is at high risk of computerisation over the coming decades. Susceptibility was highest for routine and codifiable tasks and was negatively related to wages and educational attainment. They identified perception and manipulation, creative intelligence and social intelligence as the main bottlenecks to computerisation.

Their findings are directly relevant to GRC, where regulatory monitoring, transaction screening, data aggregation and compliance reporting contain substantial routine content, while interpreting regulatory intent, challenging risk decisions and advising senior management depend on the bottleneck capabilities they describe. Read together with Autor (2015), the study suggests that GRC exposure should be assessed at the level of activities rather than whole job titles.

### 2.3.3 Technology, work and organisations (Cascio and Montealegre, 2016)

Cascio and Montealegre (2016) reviewed how technology is changing work and organisations from an organisational psychology and organisational behaviour perspective. They examined the number and types of jobs affected by technological development and the resulting worker dislocation, illustrated the effects through technologies such as electronic monitoring systems and robots, and set out implications for six areas of human resource management. Their central argument is that technological change is a matter of organisational design and HR management, not of technology alone.

For the present study, this review justifies treating AI in GRC as a workforce management question. Decisions about job design, selection, training, performance management and employee well-being determine whether the technology produces its intended benefits. It also supports the third proposition (P3), that new technology changes the skill profile organisations need.

## 2.4 REGTECH AND THE TRANSFORMATION OF COMPLIANCE

### 2.4.1 The concept of RegTech (Arner, Barberis and Buckley, 2017)

Arner, Barberis and Buckley (2017) introduced the concept of RegTech, the use of technology for regulatory monitoring, reporting and compliance. They argued that regulatory change and technological development following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis are together transforming how compliance is performed and how supervisors work. In their view, RegTech had so far largely digitised manual compliance processes, and its real potential lies in near real-time, proportionate regulation built around data, digital identity and automated reporting. This requires a reconceptualisation of financial regulation itself.

Their work establishes that compliance work is being restructured rather than merely supported by technology. If compliance shifts from periodic, sample-based checking to continuous, data-driven monitoring, the people who perform it need different skills. Arner et al. do not, however, examine those people directly; the workforce is implicit in their analysis.

### 2.4.2 The European route to RegTech (Buckley, Arner, Zetzsche and Weber, 2020)

Buckley, Arner, Zetzsche and Weber (2020) analysed the European Union's route to RegTech and identified four pillars: extensive post-crisis reporting requirements, strict data protection rules, the facilitation of open banking, and a legislative framework for digital identity. They argued that European financial services and data protection reforms have unintentionally driven the adoption of regulatory technology by banks, supervisors and regulators.

This analysis is important for the present study because it shows regulation acting as a driver of technology adoption in European compliance functions, not only as a constraint on it. Regulation shapes what is automated and, by extension, what remaining human work looks like. This supports the second proposition (P2), that regulation converts competence and oversight into compliance obligations.

## 2.5 EMPLOYEE PERCEPTIONS OF AI AND AUTOMATION

### 2.5.1 STARA awareness (Brougham and Haar, 2018)

Brougham and Haar (2018) studied employees' perceptions of smart technology, artificial intelligence, robotics and algorithms (STARA). They developed a measure of the extent to which employees believe their job could be replaced by such technologies. Using a mixed-methods design with 120 employees, they found that greater STARA awareness was negatively related to organisational commitment and career satisfaction, and positively related to turnover intentions, cynicism and depression.

Their study shows that the workforce consequences of automation are behavioural and managerial, and are not confined to headcount. Employees who expect to be replaced may disengage or leave before any role is actually removed, which is costly in GRC, where experienced staff hold knowledge that is needed to validate automated outputs. The questionnaire's perceived replaceability and organisational response constructs (Constructs D and G) are adapted from this study.

## 2.6 REGULATORY CONTEXT: WORKFORCE CAPABILITY AS AN OBLIGATION

The literature above must be read against a regulatory environment in which workforce capability has become an obligation. Four instruments are central.

- **EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), Regulation (EU) 2022/2554.** The management body bears ultimate responsibility for ICT risk and must keep its knowledge and skills up to date (Article 5). Firms must provide ICT security awareness and digital operational resilience training for all staff, including senior management (Article 13).
- **EU Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689.** Providers and deployers must take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff (Article 4). High-risk AI systems, which include creditworthiness assessment, must be subject to effective human oversight by people who understand the system's limits, are aware of automation bias and can override it (Article 14). The Digital Omnibus on AI (2026) retained the literacy duty as a duty of means and deferred some high-risk obligations.
- **PRA Supervisory Statement SS1/23, Model risk management principles for banks.** Firms must designate an accountable senior manager for the model risk framework, document roles across the model lifecycle with required skills and experience, and ensure that validators have the expertise and standing to challenge. The principles apply to AI and machine-learning models.
- **UK Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR).** There is no dedicated AI senior manager, but existing senior management functions and conduct rules apply to AI use, including the duty to take reasonable steps. AI-assisted decisions therefore remain the personal responsibility of named individuals (FCA, 2024).

Together these instruments mean that banks must be able to demonstrate that their staff are competent to understand, challenge and supervise AI-driven risk and compliance outputs. Competence and human oversight are no longer discretionary HR preferences; they are conditions of regulatory compliance.

## 2.8 RESEARCH GAP

The literature establishes three positions that have not yet been brought together.

1. **The RegTech literature** (Arner et al., 2017; Buckley et al., 2020) explains how compliance functions and regulation are being transformed by technology, but treats the workforce only implicitly.
2. **The work and employment literature** (Autor, 2015; Cascio & Montealegre, 2016; Frey & Osborne, 2017; Brougham & Haar, 2018) establishes that automation recomposes rather than removes jobs, that routine tasks are most exposed, and that perceived replaceability carries measurable costs in commitment and turnover. It is, however, cross-industry rather than specific to banking GRC.
3. **The technology acceptance and trust literature** (Davis, 1989; Glikson & Woolley, 2020) identifies the individual-level determinants of adoption but, as Glikson and Woolley note, rests largely on experimental settings rather than high-stakes regulated environments.

The gap addressed by this study lies at the intersection of the three: how AI-driven transformation of GRC in UK and EU banks is reshaping workforce management decisions on role design, skills and staffing, and how employees in a regulated, accountability-bound function adapt to it. No reviewed source combines these perspectives, and none considers how DORA, the AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR convert workforce capability into a regulatory requirement.
