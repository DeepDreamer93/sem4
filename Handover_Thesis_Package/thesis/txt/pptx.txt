1. Need for the Study

Banks in the UK and EU are investing heavily in AI across Governance, Risk and Compliance - monitoring, screening, risk scoring, control testing, reporting.
Returns depend less on the technology than on the readiness of the workforce operating alongside it.
This is a management problem, not a technical one:
– Which GRC roles are redesigned, reduced or newly created?
– What skills must be built internally or bought in the market?
– How are teams structured and staffed?
– How is employee trust in AI-enabled controls secured?
Regulation raises the stakes: DORA, the EU AI Act, PRA SS1/23 and the UK SM&CR make staff competence and human oversight conditions of compliance, not HR preferences.
A bank that automates a control without capable people to challenge the output has not reduced its risk - it has relocated it.

2. Review of Literature

Eight sources from management, organisational and financial regulation literature.
Technology acceptance and trust:
– Davis (1989), MIS Quarterly - perceived usefulness and ease of use determine use; usefulness is the stronger determinant.
– Glikson & Woolley (2020), Academy of Management Annals - integrating AI depends critically on workers’ trust; cognitive trust rests on transparency, reliability and immediacy.
Automation and the recomposition of work:
– Autor (2015), Journal of Economic Perspectives - automation substitutes for some tasks and complements others; roles are recomposed, not simply eliminated.
– Frey & Osborne (2017), Technological Forecasting and Social Change - ~47% of US employment at risk; routine, codifiable tasks most exposed.
Work, skills and organisational design:
– Cascio & Montealegre (2016), Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior - technological change as an HR and organisational design problem, with implications across six HR areas.
– Brougham & Haar (2018), Journal of Management & Organization - STARA awareness (n=120): higher awareness lowers commitment and career satisfaction, raises turnover intention and cynicism.
RegTech and the restructuring of compliance:
– Arner, Barberis & Buckley (2017) - RegTech reconceptualises financial regulation; compliance work itself is restructured, not merely supported.
– Buckley, Arner, Zetzsche & Weber (2020), Journal of Banking Regulation - four EU pillars unintentionally drove RegTech adoption; regulation as driver, not only constraint.

--- slide 4
Research Gap

RegTech literature explains how compliance and regulation are being transformed - but treats the workforce only implicitly.
Work and employment literature explains how automation recomposes jobs and how employees react - but cross-industry, not within regulated banking functions.
Acceptance and trust literature explains individual adoption - but, as Glikson and Woolley themselves note, rests largely on short-term experimental studies rather than longer-term, higher-stakes field environments.
The gap: how AI-driven transformation of GRC in UK and EU banks reshapes workforce management decisions on role design, skills and staffing - and how employees in a regulated, accountability-bound function adapt to it.
Banking GRC under SM&CR personal accountability is precisely the high-stakes field setting the trust literature identifies as under-researched.

3.1 Primary Objective

To examine how AI-driven transformation in the GRC functions of UK and EU banks is reshaping workforce management decisions - role structure, skill requirements, staffing and employee adaptation -
and to develop a practical framework enabling banking management to plan GRC workforce transformation while sustaining compliance effectiveness and employee engagement.
--- slide 6
3.2 Secondary Objectives

(i) Map which GRC activities are being automated or augmented by AI, and how this changes demand for compliance and risk roles.  [Autor; Frey & Osborne]
(ii) Analyse how UK and EU requirements - DORA, EU AI Act, SS1/23, SM&CR - shape role design, human oversight and competence obligations.  [Arner et al.; Buckley et al.]
(iii) Identify emerging skill gaps and reskilling strategies, and how management is addressing them.  [Cascio & Montealegre]
(iv) Examine employee behavioural responses - perceived usefulness, trust, perceived replaceability, readiness to adapt.  [Davis; Brougham & Haar; Glikson & Woolley]
(v) Develop an integrated workforce transformation framework and recommendations for GRC and HR leadership.
Each objective is derived from specific reviewed literature.

4. Methodology - Data Requirement and Sources

Mixed-methods design, qualitatively dominant.
Data required:
– Evidence of AI adoption in GRC activities
– Evidence of role, structure and skill change
– UK and EU regulatory requirements on competence and human oversight
– Employee and managerial perceptions
Secondary sources (all publicly available):
– Annual reports, Pillar 3 and operational resilience disclosures of 8–10 large UK and EU banks over a common three-year window
– DORA, EU AI Act, EBA and ECB publications, PRA SS1/23, FCA publications on AI and the SM&CR
– Publicly advertised GRC job postings, coded for stated skill requirements - observable evidence of changing skill demand
– Publicly available industry surveys on AI adoption and compliance workforce trends
--- slide 8
4. Methodology - Instruments, Sampling and Tools

Instruments:
– Semi-structured interview guide - ~10 open questions across AI use, task and role change, skills, and team response
– Structured questionnaire - ~24 five-point Likert items, derived from interview themes, with constructs adapted from Davis (1989), Brougham & Haar (2018) and Glikson & Woolley (2020)
Sampling framework: purposive with snowball referral - risk, compliance, audit, financial crime, GRC technology and HR roles in UK and EU banking entities; minimum two years’ experience and direct exposure to AI-enabled GRC processes.
Sample size: 12 semi-structured interviews to thematic saturation; 30 questionnaire responses as validation of the themes.
Tools: thematic analysis; content analysis of disclosures and job postings; descriptive statistics in Excel or SPSS or python; reliability via Cronbach’s alpha; triangulation of the three evidence streams.
Ethics: voluntary participation, informed consent, full anonymity, and no confidential, proprietary or client data.
