# CHAPTER 3 - METHODOLOGY

## 3.1 TYPE OF PROJECT

The project is an exploratory, independent research study. It is not a company project, and no internal bank documentation or workforce data were used. The study follows a **sequential exploratory mixed-methods design**, in which qualitative evidence is collected first and is then used to frame quantitative tests on a wider population. The design runs in three steps:

1. **Interviews and questionnaire set the hypotheses.** Written semi-structured interviews with GRC practitioners and senior professionals produced ten themes (T1–T10). A structured questionnaire then measured how strongly practitioners agree with those themes, reported as descriptive means and standard deviations.
2. **Public data test the hypotheses.** Each testable theme was translated into a hypothesis (H5–H10), which was tested statistically on independent public industry data: Eurostat enterprise statistics, bank annual reports, job adverts and published industry surveys (datasets D1–D4).
3. **Triangulation.** Practitioner views, public-data results, regulatory analysis and the reviewed literature are compared side by side to assess four research propositions (P1–P4) and to build the workforce transformation framework.

The statistical tests therefore answer one question: do the patterns that practitioners describe also hold across the wider industry? The interviews and questionnaire decided which tests were run. The public datasets are independent sources and were not produced from the primary data. This design suits the research problem, which is exploratory, and the practical constraint that banks do not publicly disclose workforce practices at the level of detail required.

## 3.3 TARGET RESPONDENTS

The population is professionals working in or with GRC functions in banks and banking service providers, including global capability centres, operating in the UK and the EU. This covers risk, compliance, internal audit, financial crime, GRC technology, and HR business partners supporting these functions.

The respondents reached span internal audit, risk, GRC technology, operational resilience, enterprise and data architecture supporting GRC service lines, and senior consulting leadership. They include analyst, manager and senior manager levels, and their responsibilities cover the UK, the EU or both. Reported AI maturity in respondents' areas ranged from no AI use (with established automation), through pilots only, to AI used in some processes. No respondent reported extensive use.

## 3.4 SAMPLING METHODS

### 3.4.1 Primary data

**Purposive sampling, supplemented by snowball referral,** was used. Purposive sampling is appropriate because the study requires respondents with direct exposure to AI-enabled GRC processes. A random sample of banking employees would largely comprise people unable to answer the questions. The selection criteria were:

i. experience in a GRC or GRC-supporting role;
ii. current or recent involvement with UK or EU banking; and
iii. direct or supervisory exposure to AI-enabled or automated GRC processes.

Interviews were continued until no materially new themes emerged on the core questions, which indicates thematic saturation within a homogeneous senior professional group. The questionnaire serves as a validation of the interview themes and not as a basis for statistical generalisation. The primary data therefore support analytical rather than statistical generalisation.

### 3.4.2 Secondary data used for statistical testing

Four public datasets were assembled to test the hypotheses. Their coverage is shown in Table 3.2.

Table 3.2 Public datasets used for hypothesis testing

| Dataset | Source | Unit and coverage | Role in the study |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Eurostat, enterprise AI use (isoc_eb_ai) and ICT specialists in employment (isoc_sks_itspt) | 27 EU countries; 2021, 2023, 2024 and 2025 | Economy-wide AI adoption and ICT-skills context |
| D2 | Annual reports from banks' official investor-relations websites | Frame of 29 large UK and EU banks (7 UK, 22 EU); FY2022 and FY2025. 42 of 58 report-years coded; 19 banks with both years | Change in AI, reskilling, AI-governance and regulation disclosure |
| D3 | Reed.co.uk job adverts | 163 UK financial-services GRC adverts, of which 83 state an annual salary | Skills and pay in AI-exposed roles |
| D4 | Published surveys: Bank of England and FCA (2022, 2024), IIA Vision 2035, Thomson Reuters Future of Professionals 2025 | Published proportions with their sample sizes | Industry adoption and skills-constraint evidence |

**D1** is a census of EU member states, so no sampling was needed. The Eurostat survey excludes the financial sector, so D1 is used as economy-wide context rather than as direct banking evidence.

**D2** uses a purposive frame of 29 of the largest UK and EU banks, fixed before data collection. For each bank, the full English annual report (or universal registration document) for FY2022 and FY2025 was taken from the bank's own investor-relations website. Every file was checked against its title page to confirm the bank and fiscal year, and mismatched files were rejected. Report-years for which no full English report was available, or whose website blocked automated access, were recorded with a reason and excluded. This produced 42 coded report-years and 19 banks with both years, which form the paired sample. Banks are shown by code in the analysis tables, and the banks included are listed in Appendix 3.

**D3** was collected from the public Reed.co.uk job board using fixed GRC search terms (for example, AI risk, compliance and financial crime roles), taking the first results pages for each term, in October 2026. Only financial-services adverts were kept, and employer names were not recorded.

**D4** consists of published proportions and sample sizes from public industry and supervisory surveys. No new survey data were collected.

## 3.5 RESEARCH INSTRUMENTS

### 3.5.1 Semi-structured interview guide

The interview guide contains eleven open-ended questions in five sections: (1) current use of AI in GRC work; (2) changes in role content and team structure; (3) regulation, human oversight and accountability; (4) skills and reskilling; and (5) employee response and the management of change, followed by a closing open question. Because of respondents' seniority and availability, the guide was administered as a written interview through an online form. This allowed considered, detailed answers. The form is reproduced in Appendix 1.

### 3.5.2 Structured questionnaire

The questionnaire has a profile section (function, level, experience, jurisdiction, type of organisation and extent of AI use), followed by 25 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree) and two optional open questions. The items are grouped into seven constructs, shown in Table 3.3. The full questionnaire is reproduced in Appendix 2.

## 3.6 RESEARCH PROPOSITIONS AND HYPOTHESES

### 3.6.1 Research propositions

Because the design is exploratory and qualitatively dominant, the study is guided by four research propositions, assessed through triangulation (Table 3.5).

Table 3.5 Research propositions

| No. | Proposition | Main evidence |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Routine, rules-based GRC tasks are automated first, while judgement-intensive tasks are augmented rather than replaced. | Interviews; job-description analysis; H5, H8, H10a |
| P2 | Regulation converts workforce competence and human oversight into compliance obligations. | Regulatory texts; interviews; H10a (regulation), H10b, H10d |
| P3 | AI adoption in GRC creates demand for a hybrid skill profile combining technical and data fluency with domain and regulatory judgement. | Interviews; Construct E; H5b, H6, H7, H9 |
| P4 | Loss of institutional knowledge is a material risk of AI-led workforce restructuring in GRC. | Interviews (emergent theme); secondary evidence |

### 3.6.2 Questionnaire hypotheses (descriptive)

Four directional hypotheses are assessed on the questionnaire by comparing the construct or item mean with the neutral scale midpoint of 3:

- **H1:** GRC professionals perceive AI-enabled tools as useful (Construct A mean > 3).
- **H2:** GRC professionals perceive AI-enabled tools as easy to use (Construct B mean > 3).
- **H3:** GRC professionals expect their roles to change substantially because of AI (item 20 mean > 3).
- **H4:** Trust in AI output is conditional: comfort in relying on output without re-performing the work (item 17) is rated lower than the reliability of the output (item 15).

The questionnaire was administered to a small, purposive group of senior practitioners as a validation instrument. With a sample of this kind, significance tests and reliability coefficients such as Cronbach's alpha would not be meaningful. H1–H4 are therefore assessed descriptively, and the statistical testing in this study is performed on the public datasets.

## 3.7 DATA PROCESSING

**Interviews.** Written responses were read in full and open-coded line by line. Codes were grouped into themes aligned to the secondary objectives, and illustrative verbatim quotations were selected for each theme. Coding was performed manually in Microsoft Excel.

**Questionnaire.** Responses were exported from the online form. Item and construct means and standard deviations were calculated. Construct D is reported in the direction of concern (a higher score means more concern). Item 30 (considering leaving) is reverse-coded within Construct G so that a higher score means a more positive organisational response. Responses were screened for uniform answering using the reverse-worded items, and results are interpreted with that caution in mind.

**D1.** Country-level values were downloaded from the Eurostat API for enterprises with 10 or more employees and with 250 or more employees.

**D2.** Each annual report was converted from PDF to text, and fixed, case-insensitive regular expressions were run over the full text to count four groups of terms: AI, reskilling, AI governance and regulation (the AI Act, DORA and SS1/23). Each count was divided by the report's total word count and multiplied by 10,000, giving a density per 10,000 words, so that long and short reports can be compared fairly. A binary variable records whether the report names a dedicated AI governance body or role. The coding dictionary is given in Appendix 3, and the variables are defined in Table 3.8.

**D3.** Advert text was flagged for mentions of AI or machine learning ("AI-exposed") and for six skill groups: data, regulatory, cyber, automation, certification and governance. For pay analysis, the midpoint of the advertised annual salary was used. Daily and hourly rates were excluded.

**D4.** Published proportions and their sample sizes were recorded with the page reference of the source statement. Items without a published sample size were reported descriptively and not tested.

Table 3.8 Variables used in the statistical analysis

| Variable | Definition |
|---|---|
| AI adoption, 10+ / 250+ (D1) | % of enterprises with 10+ / 250+ employees using at least one AI technology |
| ICT specialists (D1) | ICT specialists as % of total employment |
| AI density (D2) | Mentions of AI terms (artificial intelligence, machine learning, generative AI, large language models, "AI") per 10,000 words |
| Reskilling density (D2) | Mentions of reskilling, upskilling, retraining, digital skills, AI or data literacy, skills gap or development per 10,000 words |
| AI-governance density (D2) | Mentions of AI governance, responsible or ethical AI, human oversight, human-in-the-loop, explainability and model risk per 10,000 words |
| Regulation density (D2) | Mentions of the EU AI Act, DORA or digital operational resilience, and PRA SS1/23 per 10,000 words |
| AI governance body (D2) | 1 if the report names a dedicated AI governance body or role (e.g. chief data or AI officer, AI council or committee, responsible-AI office), else 0 |
| AI-exposed (D3) | 1 if the advert mentions AI or machine learning |
| Skill flags (D3) | 1 if the advert asks for data, regulatory, cyber, automation, certification or governance skills |
| Annual salary (D3) | Midpoint of the advertised annual salary (£) |

## 3.8 TOOLS FOR ANALYSIS

The qualitative and descriptive analysis used Microsoft Excel. The statistical analysis used Python (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels and matplotlib), and all results can be reproduced from the project's analysis script. The significance level is α = 0.05. Tests are one-sided where the hypothesis predicts a direction. The choice of test for each hypothesis is explained below.

- **Thematic analysis** (interviews): open coding grouped into themes by objective.
- **Content analysis** (regulatory texts, supervisory publications and job descriptions): activities coded as automated, augmented or unaffected, and skills coded as technical, analytical, regulatory or behavioural.
- **Descriptive statistics** (questionnaire): item and construct means and standard deviations compared with the midpoint of 3.
- **Paired t-test with Wilcoxon signed-rank test** (H5a, H10a): the same countries or banks are measured twice, so the comparison is paired. The t-test compares mean change, and the Wilcoxon test confirms the result without assuming normality, which matters for small, skewed samples such as D2 densities.
- **Friedman test** (H5a): a non-parametric repeated-measures test across all four Eurostat years.
- **Pearson and Spearman correlation and simple OLS regression** (H5b): these measure the strength of the link between ICT skills and AI adoption. Spearman is the rank-based check.
- **Chi-square or Fisher exact test with Holm correction** (H6): skill flags are yes/no variables. Fisher's test is used when expected cell counts are small, and the Holm correction controls for testing six skills at once.
- **Mann–Whitney U test with Welch t-test** (H7, H10d): these compare two independent groups. Salaries and densities are skewed and the groups are small, so the rank test is the main test. The Holm correction is applied across the four D2 densities.
- **Two-proportion and one-sample z-tests** (H8, H9): only published proportions and sample sizes are available.
- **Exact McNemar test** (H10b): a paired yes/no outcome for the same banks in two years.
- **Spearman rank correlation** (H10c): a rank correlation that is robust to outliers in a small sample.
- **Triangulation**: the evidence streams are compared side by side for each proposition. The questionnaire and the public data measure different units (individuals, as against countries, banks and adverts), so they are compared, not pooled into one test.

## 3.9 ASSUMPTIONS, CONSTRAINTS AND LIMITATIONS

**Assumptions.** Respondents answered honestly and from their professional experience. The language of bank annual reports reflects, at least in part, the priorities of bank management. Job adverts reflect the skills employers are currently seeking.

**Constraints.** The study was conducted without institutional access, so no internal bank data were available. Senior respondents had limited time, which is why the interviews were collected in written form. Some bank websites block automated download, which limited the number of paired bank reports.

**Limitations.**

1. **Primary sample.** The interviews and questionnaire come from a small, purposive group with a technology-leaning profile. They support analytical, not statistical, generalisation, and the questionnaire results are descriptive only.
2. **Self-reported perceptions.** Trust, usefulness and replaceability are self-reported and may be affected by social desirability.
3. **D1** covers the whole economy and excludes the financial sector, so it is context only.
4. **D2** counts words, not actions. A mention of AI or reskilling does not show what a bank actually does, and results depend on report length, language and PDF text extraction. Only 19 of the 29 banks had both years available, and UK banks are few (six in FY2025), so the regional tests have low power. One bank's financial year ends in April and is mapped to the nearest year.
5. **D3** comes from one UK job board on a single collection date (a convenience sample). It contains no EU adverts and few AI-titled adverts, so the pay result is indicative only.
6. **D4** is secondary published data, and survey waves use different samples.
7. **Multiple testing.** Holm corrections are applied within families of tests (H6, H10d), but other p-values are unadjusted.
8. **Cross-sectional design and fast-moving context.** Primary data capture one point in an ongoing transition. The AI Act is being phased in and has been amended, so specific requirements may change.
9. **Jurisdictional breadth.** The UK and EU are treated as one combined scope, so national differences within the EU are not examined.

## 3.10 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Participation was voluntary, and every respondent gave informed consent to the use of anonymised responses. No respondent, employer or client is identified in this report. Respondents were asked to speak to general practice and not to share confidential or proprietary information. Primary data are stored securely and used only for academic purposes. All secondary data are publicly available. Banks are named only in Appendix 3, in connection with their published annual reports.
