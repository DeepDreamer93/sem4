## 1. NEED FOR THE STUDY

Banks in the United Kingdom and the European Union are investing substantially in Artificial Intelligence (AI) across their Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC) functions, applying it to regulatory monitoring, customer and transaction screening, risk scoring, control testing and compliance reporting. The business case is usually framed around cost efficiency and faster regulatory response. In practice, however, the returns depend less on the capability of the technology than on the readiness of the workforce that must operate alongside it.

Evidence gathered since the first review confirms the scale and urgency of the issue. The joint Bank of England and FCA survey of AI in UK financial services found that 75% of firms are already using AI, with a further 10% planning to do so within three years; that one third of AI use cases are third-party implementations, up from 17% in the previous survey; and that risk and compliance is among the business areas with the highest share of third-party implementations, at 64% (Bank of England & FCA, 2024). The same survey found that 46% of firms have only a "partial understanding" of the AI technologies they use, and that insufficient talent and access to skills is a large or medium constraint for more than half of firms. In other words, adoption is moving faster than the workforce capability needed to govern it.

This creates a management problem rather than a technical one. When AI absorbs routine monitoring and reporting work, management must decide which GRC roles are redesigned, reduced or newly created; which skills must be built internally or bought in the market; how teams should be structured and staffed; and how employees can be brought to trust and use AI-enabled controls rather than work around them. The labour market is already signalling this shift: publicly advertised AI risk and compliance roles grew by 187% year-on-year while general technology hiring contracted, with financial services and banking among the leading hiring sectors (Global Skill Development Council, 2026).

The regulatory environment raises the stakes further. The EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the EU Artificial Intelligence Act impose requirements for operational resilience, staff competence, AI literacy and meaningful human oversight. In the United Kingdom, PRA Supervisory Statement SS1/23 sets expectations for model risk management, including the skills of those who develop and validate models, and the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) holds named individuals personally accountable for decisions that are increasingly AI-assisted. Staff competence and effective human oversight have therefore become regulatory conditions, not merely HR objectives.

Existing research does not address this intersection. The RegTech literature explains how technology is reshaping compliance but treats the workforce only implicitly; the work and employment literature explains how automation recomposes jobs, but across industries rather than within regulated banking functions; and the technology acceptance and trust literature explains individual adoption largely through experimental rather than high-stakes settings. This study is therefore undertaken to examine AI-driven transformation in UK and EU banking GRC through a workforce management lens, and to develop an actionable framework that banking management can use to plan role redesign, reskilling and employee adaptation while sustaining both compliance effectiveness and employee engagement.

## 2 (a). PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

To examine how AI-driven transformation in the Governance, Risk and Compliance functions of UK and EU banks is reshaping workforce management decisions - role structure, skill requirements, staffing and employee adaptation - and to develop a practical framework enabling banking management to plan GRC workforce transformation while sustaining compliance effectiveness and employee engagement.

**Changes since the first review:** The primary objective is unchanged.

## 2 (b). SECONDARY OBJECTIVES

1. To map which GRC activities are being automated or augmented by AI in UK and EU banks, and how this is changing the demand for compliance and risk roles.
2. To analyse how UK and EU regulatory requirements - DORA, the EU AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR - shape role design, human oversight and competence obligations within GRC functions.
3. To identify emerging skill gaps and reskilling strategies in GRC functions, and to assess how they are being addressed by management.
4. To examine employee behavioural responses to AI adoption in GRC - perceived usefulness, trust, perceived replaceability and readiness to adapt - and their implications for adoption and retention.
5. To develop an integrated workforce transformation framework and recommendations for GRC and HR leadership in UK and EU banks.

## 3. METHODOLOGY

### 3.1 Research Design
The study adopts a **mixed-methods design, qualitatively dominant**, combining analysis of publicly available secondary data with primary data from written semi-structured practitioner interviews and a structured follow-up questionnaire. The design reflects the exploratory nature of the research problem and the fact that banks do not publicly disclose workforce practices at the level of detail required. Evidence from the three streams (secondary data, interviews, questionnaire) is triangulated before conclusions are drawn.

### 3.2 Data Requirement
| Data category | Source | Objective served |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Evidence of AI adoption in GRC: activities automated or augmented | Secondary (supervisory surveys, industry reports) + interviews | (i) |
| 2. Evidence of role and skill change: job content, new roles, skill demand | Secondary (public job postings, labour-market and professional-body reports) + interviews | (i), (iii) |
| 3. Regulatory requirements on workforce competence and human oversight | Secondary (UK and EU regulatory and supervisory texts) | (ii) |
| 4. Employee and managerial perceptions: usefulness, ease of use, trust, replaceability, skills, oversight, organisational response | Primary (interviews + questionnaire) | (iii), (iv) |

### 3.3 Research Instruments
**(a) Semi-structured interview guide.** Eleven open-ended questions in four blocks: (1) current use of AI and automation in GRC; (2) changes in tasks, role content and team shape, including regulatory oversight; (3) skills, capability to challenge AI output, and reskilling approaches; (4) employee responses and management of the transition, with a closing open question. Given respondents' seniority and availability, the guide was administered as a **written interview** through an online form, allowing respondents to give considered, detailed answers.

**(b) Structured questionnaire.** A profile section (function, level, experience, jurisdiction, type of organisation, extent of AI use) followed by **25 Likert items** on a five-point scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree) and two open questions. Items are grouped into seven constructs:

| Construct | Items | Adapted from |
|---|---|---|
| A. Perceived usefulness | 7-10 | Davis (1989) |
| B. Perceived ease of use | 11-13 | Davis (1989) |
| C. Trust in AI output | 14-18 | Glikson & Woolley (2020) |
| D. Perceived replaceability | 19-21 | Brougham & Haar (2018) |
| E. Skills and reskilling | 22-25 | Study-specific |
| F. Human oversight and accountability | 26-27 | Study-specific, grounded in DORA / AI Act / SS1/23 / SM&CR |
| G. Organisational response and outcomes | 28-31 | Brougham & Haar (2018) |

Items 19-21 and 30 are worded so that a higher score indicates greater concern; these are reverse-coded when composite scores are calculated.

**Method of preparing the instruments.** The interview guide was derived from the secondary objectives and the gaps identified in the literature review. The questionnaire was then built from the interview themes using validated constructs from the reviewed literature rather than newly invented scales, so that reliability can be assessed and findings compared with published work. Each instrument item is mapped to an objective (Annexure), and both instruments include an informed-consent statement.

### 3.6 Research Propositions and Hypotheses
Because the design is exploratory and qualitatively dominant, the study is guided by research propositions that are tested through triangulation, with directional hypotheses on the questionnaire constructs tested against the neutral midpoint of the scale (3).

| # | Proposition / hypothesis | Test applied |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Routine, rules-based GRC tasks are automated first, while judgement-intensive tasks are augmented rather than replaced. | Thematic analysis + content analysis of job postings |
| P2 | Regulation converts workforce competence and human oversight into compliance obligations. | Content analysis of regulatory texts + interviews |
| P3 | AI adoption in GRC creates demand for a hybrid skill profile combining technical/data fluency with domain and regulatory judgement. | Interviews + job-posting skill coding + questionnaire construct E |
| P4 | Loss of institutional knowledge is a material risk of AI-led workforce restructuring in GRC. | Interviews (emergent) + secondary evidence |
| H1 | GRC professionals perceive AI-enabled tools as useful (Construct A mean > 3). | Descriptive mean vs midpoint; one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the final data file |
| H2 | GRC professionals perceive AI-enabled tools as easy to use (Construct B mean > 3). | As above |
| H3 | GRC professionals expect their roles to change substantially because of AI (Item 20 mean > 3). | As above |
| H4 | Trust in AI output is conditional: reliance without re-performance (Item 17) is rated lower than reliability (Item 15). | Paired comparison of item means; Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the final data file |

### 3.7 Tools and Techniques of Analysis
- **Thematic analysis** of interview responses: open coding, then grouping of codes into themes aligned to the secondary objectives (manual coding in Microsoft Excel).
- **Content analysis** of regulatory texts, supervisory publications and public job postings: activities coded as automated, augmented or unaffected; skill requirements coded as technical, analytical, regulatory or behavioural.
- **Descriptive statistics** for the questionnaire: item and construct means and standard deviations (Microsoft Excel / Python).
- **Reliability assessment** of multi-item constructs using Cronbach's alpha.
- **Non-parametric tests** (one-sample and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank) suitable for ordinal Likert data and small purposive samples.
- **Triangulation** of all three evidence streams to test convergence before conclusions are drawn.
- **Framework development**, integrating the validated themes with the reviewed literature.

### 3.8 Ethical Considerations
Participation was voluntary and every respondent gave informed consent to the use of anonymised responses. No respondent, employer, client or bank is identified in this report. No confidential or proprietary data was collected, and respondents were asked to speak to general practice. Data is stored securely and used only for academic purposes.

**Key inferences from the questionnaire**
1. **Usability-transparency gap.** Ease of use (items 11, 13: 4.33) is clearly higher than understanding what the tool is doing (item 12: 3.33). Tools are easy to operate but not easy to see into - consistent with interview theme T10 and with the transparency dimension of cognitive trust (Glikson & Woolley, 2020).
2. **Conditional trust (H4).** Confidence in defending an AI-assisted decision (4.33) is higher than comfort in relying on AI output without re-performing work (3.67), and the latter shows wider dispersion. Practitioners keep a human check in place even when they rate outputs as reliable - supporting T4.
3. **Change is accepted as inevitable (H3).** Expectation that roles will change substantially (item 20: 4.33, lowest dispersion) is the strongest replaceability item, while concern that one's own role could be performed by AI (item 19) is the most dispersed item in the instrument - consistent with T10, where anxiety is concentrated among junior staff.
4. **Training lags skill demand.** Rising importance of data skills and of judgement (items 24-25: 4.00) is not matched by adequacy of training (item 23: 3.33, high dispersion) - supporting T7 and T9.
5. **Oversight exists but is not yet operationalised.** Knowledge of accountable persons (item 26: 4.00) is rated higher than the clarity with which regulatory oversight requirements are reflected in practice (item 27: 3.67) - supporting T5 and T6.
6. **Perceived usefulness depends on maturity.** Usefulness scores were lowest where AI was only piloted and higher where it was used in processes - consistent with T1 and with the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989).
7. **Open-ended responses** identified human review and oversight as the part of GRC work that should not be automated, and dedicated learning modules for each regulation within every domain as the single change that would most help staff adapt.

**Data-quality note.** One response gave the highest rating on every item from Construct C onwards, including logically opposing items (item 30, considering leaving, and item 31, commitment). This is characteristic of uniform responding; the response is retained but interpreted with caution and will be screened using the reverse-worded items in the final analysis.

| Agentic AI in KYC/AML | Human effort shifts to exception handling, validation oversight, customer interaction and final decision-making under a human-in-the-loop design | IBM (2026) |

*Inference:* GRC teams are moving from a broad base of junior processors toward a narrower base and a wider middle of experienced reviewers and specialists ("pyramid to diamond"). Combined with the lower engagement of early-career professionals, this raises a talent-pipeline question: how will future experts be developed if entry-level processing roles shrink?

### 5.6 Preliminary Workforce Transformation Framework
| Stage | Management decision | Key actions | Grounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose | Which activities are exposed and which people hold critical knowledge? | Activity-level mapping (automated / augmented / retained); AI inventory; identify knowledge-critical roles | P1, T2, T8 |
| 2. Protect knowledge | How is tacit expertise preserved before change? | Knowledge capture, procedure codification, mentoring; no material reductions until capture is complete | P4, T8 |
| 3. Redesign roles | Which roles are redesigned, reduced or created? | Rewrite job descriptions toward review, challenge and system ownership; create AI governance, validation and assurance roles; protect developmental entry roles | 5.3.3, 5.5 |
| 4. Build oversight competence | Can accountable people meaningfully challenge AI? | Tiered AI literacy (staff, overseers, model owners, board); competence matrix linked to DORA, AI Act, SS1/23, SM&CR; evidence of training | P2, T5, 5.3.2 |
| 5. Reskill and source | Build, buy or borrow? | Build hybrid skills through regulation-by-domain modules and embedded prompts; buy scarce AI-risk specialists; borrow vendor capacity under DORA third-party controls | P3, T7, T9 |
| 6. Secure trust and adoption | Will staff rely on AI appropriately? | Explainability at point of use; clear override routes; demonstrate usefulness in daily work | H1, H4, T10 |
| 7. Lead change and retain talent | How are engagement and commitment sustained? | Transparent communication on role impact; career pathways from processing to investigation, QA and data roles; targeted support for junior staff | H3, T10, Construct G |

**Cross-cutting enablers:** data quality and lineage; clear accountability mapping; continuous monitoring of AI performance and workforce indicators.

### 5.7 Summary of Findings Against Objectives
| Objective | Key finding |
|---|---|
| (i) | Automation of routine, rules-based GRC work is established; AI is largely augmentative and maturing; execution and testing roles contract first, while review, investigation and AI governance roles grow. |
| (ii) | DORA, the EU AI Act, SS1/23 and SM&CR convert competence and human oversight into obligations, but these have not yet been fully translated into role-level practice; senior-level understanding of AI is the critical weak point. |
| (iii) | A hybrid technical-plus-domain profile is required; gaps are largest in AI risk, analytics and risk quantification; training lags demand; institutional knowledge loss is a major hidden risk. |
| (iv) | Ease of use drives adoption, but trust is conditional on transparency and the ability to validate; change is accepted as inevitable, while anxiety concentrates among junior staff and change management is weak. |
| (v) | A seven-stage, sequenced workforce transformation framework is proposed, with knowledge protection preceding restructuring. |
